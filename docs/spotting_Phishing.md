\# How to Spot Phishing: A Guide to Recognizing Scams



Phishing is a type of social engineering attack used by malicious actors to steal sensitive data such as login credentials, credit card information, and more. It remains one of the biggest cybersecurity threats organizations face today — according to Proofpoint's 2021 State of the Phish Report, \*\*80% of organizations fell for phishing attacks in 2021\*\*.



Phishing attacks have been around for decades, but their frequency and effectiveness have skyrocketed recently. The good news? The more aware you are of the warning signs, the less likely you are to fall victim.



\---



\## Common Types of Phishing Attacks



| Type | Description |

|------|-------------|

| \*\*Email Phishing\*\* | Sent via email with a sense of urgency, asking you to click a link that redirects to a fake (cloned) website designed to steal login credentials. |

| \*\*Spear Phishing\*\* | Targeted at a specific individual. Attackers gather basic information (name, employer, job title, email) to make the attack more convincing. |

| \*\*Whaling\*\* | Targets senior executives, often using the pretext of a busy executive asking an employee for a favor. |

| \*\*Smishing \& Vishing\*\* | Conducted via telephone. Smishing comes as a text message (e.g., fake bank alerts); vishing comes as a phone call. |



\---



\## Red Flags: How to Spot a Phishing Attempt



\### 1. The Sender Requests Your Credentials

One of the most obvious signs of a phishing scam is when an email asks for your \*\*login credentials or financial information\*\*. Legitimate companies will not ask you to enter your username, password, or credit card details via email. Be suspicious of:

\- "Customer service" emails from banks or utilities

\- Emails from companies you have an order with

\- Emails from companies you don't have an account with at all



\### 2. The Email Asks You to Click a Link

Threat actors craft emails to appear from a trusted source (e.g., your bank) and entice you to click a link — often claiming you need to "update your account information." That link may lead to a phishing website that steals your information, or even give attackers full control of your device via a \*\*zero-click attack\*\*.



> \*\*Tip:\*\* If an email directs you to click a link, contact the company through their legitimate phone number or website to verify authenticity.



\### 3. The Language Is Awkward or Poorly Written

Phishing emails often:

\- Fail to address you by name (using "Dear User," "Dear Customer," or just "Hello")

\- Use an overly formal or corporate tone

\- Contain spelling or grammatical mistakes

\- Have a tone that just feels "off"



Legitimate companies you do business with will typically address you by your name.



\### 4. You Don't Recognize the Sender

If you receive an email from a company you don't do business with — or from a person you don't know — scrutinize it carefully. Also watch for:

\- A wrong address or contact name

\- A sender name that differs from what's listed on your account



\### 5. You Weren't Expecting the Email

A sure-fire sign of phishing is receiving an email you \*\*didn't expect or request\*\*. Companies won't send you an email out of the blue and expect you to know what they want. If something needs to be done or updated, legitimate organizations will typically contact you through another means, such as a phone call or postal mail.



\---



\## Quick Reference Checklist



\- \[ ] Does the email ask for login credentials or financial info?

\- \[ ] Does it ask you to click a suspicious link?

\- \[ ] Is the language awkward, generic, or full of errors?

\- \[ ] Do you recognize the sender?

\- \[ ] Were you expecting this email?



If you answered \*\*yes\*\* to any of these — or if something just feels wrong — it probably is. When in doubt, verify through official channels before taking any action.



\---



\## Key Takeaway



> If you think an email might be a phishing attack — for example, if it asks you to give up personal information or click on a link — then it probably is. Phishing scams almost always have obvious warning signs you can see with a bit of careful consideration.



Stay alert, trust your instincts, and always verify before you click.
