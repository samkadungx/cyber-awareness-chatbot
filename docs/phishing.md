Phishing: What It Is and What It Is Not

Based on CISA/NSA/FBI/MS-ISAC joint guidance, October 2023



What Phishing Is

Phishing is a form of social engineering — an attempt to trick someone into revealing information (like a password) or taking an action that compromises systems or networks. Malicious actors typically lure victims via email to visit a malicious site or deceive them into providing login credentials.



Two Primary Goals

Obtaining login credentials — Stealing usernames/passwords for initial network access.



Malware deployment — Delivering malware for follow-on activity (interrupting systems, escalating privileges, maintaining persistence).



Common Phishing Techniques

Impersonation — Posing as supervisors, colleagues, IT personnel, or trusted organizations.



Multi-channel attacks — Using SMS, chat platforms (Slack, Teams, Signal, WhatsApp, Facebook Messenger), or VoIP caller ID spoofing.



Malicious links/attachments — Sending hyperlinks or files that download malware (often via macro scripts or obfuscated links).



Credential harvesting sites — Fake login portals that capture usernames, passwords, and MFA codes.



MFA fatigue — Bombarding users with push notifications until they accidentally approve.



SIM swapping — Convincing carriers to transfer phone numbers to intercept SMS/voice MFA codes.



Why Hybrid Environments Are More Vulnerable

Organizations with fewer face-to-face interactions and frequent virtual exchanges are more likely to be deceived by social engineering tailored to platforms they frequently use.



What Phishing Is Not

Not limited to email — It occurs via SMS, chat apps, voice calls, and social media.



Not solved by MFA alone — Weak MFA (push without number matching, SMS, voice) is still vulnerable. Only phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO or PKI-based) reliably stops credential phishing.



Not just a user problem — Organizations and software manufacturers share responsibility. Secure-by-design and secure-by-default practices are essential.



Not always obvious — Malicious URLs are hard to detect on small screens; attackers use free tools (GoPhish, Zphisher) to craft convincing lures.



Not a one-time fix — Requires continuous training, monitoring, and layered defenses.
